Belts and Chains

STANDARD BELT (Same Direction)
CROSSED BELT (Reverse Direction)
CHAIN DRIVE (No Slippage)
MANUAL SCRUB

When you need to transfer power between two shafts that are far apart, gears become impractical. Belts and Chains are used to span distances.

Key Differences:

  • Standard Belt: Smooth and quiet. Output shaft rotates in the same direction as the input. Belts can intentionally slip if the system jams, acting as a mechanical fuse.
  • Crossed Belt: By twisting the belt in a Figure-8, the output shaft will rotate in the opposite direction, eliminating the need for an idler gear.
  • Chain Drive: Uses interlocking teeth (sprockets) and chain links. Chains are louder and require lubrication, but they provide positive engagement (zero slippage), making them perfect for high-torque applications like bicycles or motorcycles.

Hydraulic Force Transmission

MANUAL PUMP

Hydraulics use incompressible fluids to transmit force. They act as a fluid gear system, obeying Pascal's Principle: pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished in every direction.

How it works:

  • Input (Small Area): A small force is applied to a small piston. It pushes the fluid a very long distance.
  • Output (Large Area): The displaced fluid pushes against a much larger piston. Because the area is larger, the resulting lifting force is massive, but the piston only moves a short distance.

Examples: Car brakes, heavy excavators, hydraulic car lifts, robotic actuators.

Circular to Reciprocating

MANUAL SCRUB

The Slider-Crank mechanism converts continuous rotational motion into linear, back-and-forth (reciprocating) motion.

How it works:

  • Driving Gear / Crank: Rotates in a continuous circle.
  • Connecting Rod: Links the rotating crank to the slider. As the crank turns, it pushes and pulls the rod.
  • Slider: Constrained within a channel, it is forced to move in a straight line back and forth.

Examples: Car engines (pistons), manual water pumps, automated saws.

Circular to Oscillation & Gait

BASIC OSCILLATION
COMPLEX GAIT (Jansen Linkage)
MANUAL SCRUB

A Four-Bar Linkage can be configured to convert circular motion into a sweeping, oscillating motion, or even a complex walking gait.

How it works:

  • Driving Crank: Makes a full continuous rotation.
  • Coupler: Connects the crank to the rocker, transmitting the force.
  • Rocker: Because of its pivot and length, it cannot complete a full circle, so it swings (oscillates) back and forth.

Examples: Windshield wipers, oil pump jacks, kinetic walking robots.

Quick Return Mechanism

MANUAL SCRUB

The Crank and Slotted Lever mechanism creates a stroke that moves slowly forward (for cutting or doing work) and rapidly backwards (to reset efficiently).

How it works:

  • Driving Crank: A small wheel that rotates at a constant speed, sliding a pin inside a lever.
  • Slotted Lever: Pivots at the bottom. It takes more than half a rotation of the crank to push the lever forward, and less than half to pull it back.
  • Ram: Attached to the top of the lever, it performs the linear work.

Examples: Shaper machines, automated assembly arms, specialized cutting tools.

Circular to Linear

MANUAL SCRUB

The Rack and Pinion mechanism provides precise conversion of rotational motion into direct linear translation along a fixed axis.

How it works:

  • Driving Gear (Pinion): A standard circular gear that rotates.
  • Rack: A flat bar with teeth cut into it. As the pinion turns, its teeth engage the rack, driving it in a straight line.

Examples: Car steering systems, CNC machine axes, sliding gates.

Timing by Cams

MANUAL SCRUB

Cam and Follower mechanisms act as physical memory. The shape of the cam physically programs exactly when and how far the follower will move.

How it works:

  • Driving Cam: A non-circular (often teardrop or egg-shaped) piece of metal that rotates on an axis.
  • Follower: A rod or lever that rests against the edge of the cam. As the "lobe" (the bump) of the cam comes around, it pushes the follower up.

Examples: Internal combustion engine valves, automated sewing machines, automata.

Gears, Speed, and Torque

1:1 TRANSFER
IDLER GEAR (Same Direction)
SPEED UP (Lose Torque)
TORQUE UP (Lose Speed)
MANUAL SCRUB

Gears are the most fundamental way to transfer rotational motion and alter the mechanical advantage of a system.

Core Rules of Gears:

  • Direction: Two meshing gears always spin in opposite directions. To make the output gear spin the same way as the input, you must insert an Idler Gear in the middle.
  • The Golden Trade-off: You cannot increase both speed and power (torque) at the same time.
  • Speed Up: A large driving gear turning a small driven gear makes the small gear spin very fast, but with very little force.
  • Torque Up: A small driving gear turning a large driven gear makes the large gear spin very slowly, but with immense crushing force.
